Nursing lets me build real relationships with patients. Becoming a family nurse practitioner is how I plan to turn that into a career.

Research

The impact of nurse-patient ratios on patient outcomes in hospital settings

University of Miami, WRS 230 Advanced Professional Communication (Honors) · December 2025

Nurses are the clinicians who watch patients most closely, and many life-threatening problems begin with subtle signs. My report asks what happens to patients when each nurse is responsible for too many of them. I synthesized nine peer-reviewed U.S. studies and one news report covering mortality, medication errors, falls, infections, surgical complications, patient satisfaction, and hospital costs, with a closer look at high-acuity ICUs and post-surgical units.

The evidence points in one direction: understaffing drives a cycle of heavier workloads, burnout, and turnover that worsens the shortage and puts patients at risk. It also costs hospitals more, not less. California's Assembly Bill 394, in effect since 2004 and still the only statewide ratio mandate, shows that setting limits improves outcomes without the financial harm hospitals feared.

Writing this paper shaped how I think about advanced practice. Safe care depends not only on individual clinicians, but on the systems and policies around them, and that is part of the work I hope to do.

Key findings from the evidence

7%
higher surgical mortality for every extra patient added to a nurse's workload (Aiken et al., 2002)
2%
higher mortality risk for each understaffed shift a patient experiences (Needleman et al., 2011)
68% / 79%
fewer central line bloodstream infections and ventilator pneumonias in better-staffed ICUs (Stone et al., 2007)
48%
more failure-to-rescue events in understaffed surgical oncology units (Friese et al., 2008)
10–14%
potential drop in mortality if other states adopted California-style ratios (Aiken et al., 2010)
